Methodology

What counts as an event

An event starts in the first month that either of two things happens, measured on today's data:

  • Claims jump: the SA initial claims 4-week average is 25% or more above its lowest value of the previous 52 weeks.
  • Unemployment rise: the unemployment rate's 3-month average is 0.5 point or more above its lowest 3-month average of the previous 12 months (the shape of the Sahm rule).

It ends once both have been off for 6 months. Since 1967 there have been 15 events: 8 linked to a recession (or an early signal of one), 6 scares, and 2020. Scares count as events: for a lender, a sharp short jump in new claims matters even if no recession follows. Since 2000, about 1 red month in 5 was followed by an event tied to a recession; the rest preceded scares or nothing.

Inputs and their weights (today)

A penalized logistic regression of "an event starts within 6 months" on eight measures, each standardized; weights are constrained to be zero or positive, re-fitted every January (this year's fit: January {{ model_year }}, using only what was known then, apart from later revisions to the claims figures). A positive contribution pushes the score up.

How the colour is set

Amber starts at {{ (thresholds.amber_in * 100) | round | int }}% and stays until the score falls below {{ (thresholds.amber_out * 100) | round | int }}%; red starts at {{ (thresholds.red_in * 100) | round | int }}% and stays until it falls below {{ (thresholds.red_out * 100) | round | int }}%. The colour is worked out from the stored history, not from this week's number alone, so it does not flicker around a threshold. The amber level was chosen on 1968–1999 data only (about one false amber per real warning) and then tested from 2000.

The long amber and red stretch of 2022–2025

From {{ backtest.evidence.run.start }} to {{ backtest.evidence.run.end }} the score was amber or red for {{ backtest.evidence.run.months }} months in a row. Two short scares followed (mid-2023 and the mid-2024 rise in unemployment) and no recession. Over the same stretch the unemployment rate rose from {{ backtest.evidence.run.ur_low }}% to {{ backtest.evidence.run.ur_high }}%: a slow drift, which this score flags but cannot tell apart from the start of something sharper. From late 2024 through 2025 the score was often elevated without a new event following. Three things drove it: temporary-help employment was falling (the largest push in most months); the Sahm indicator stayed in the 0.3–0.5 range after the mid-2024 rise in unemployment; and initial claims had several short jumps above their 52-week low, the largest in October 2024 (Hurricanes Helene and Milton and the Boeing strike) and in June and September 2025. The red reading of June 2025 (85%) combined a 13% rise in claims above their low with a 1.6% six-month fall in temp-help jobs, on top of a Sahm indicator of 0.27 and weak factory surveys; claims eased the next month and the score fell to 25%. In each case the inputs moved the way they have before past downturns, but not far or long enough. A high probability that doesn't materialise is part of how a probability behaves.

The 2022 claims-revision effect

The score is tested on today's claims figures. With claims as they were first published, its 2010–2026 skill falls from +0.20 to −0.23. Almost all of that is 2022: first-published claims rose 36–46% above their March 2022 low, against 2–6% after the Labor Department's 2023 revision of the seasonal factors. In real time the score would have been 77–99% from June 2022 to January 2023, a false red that today's figures hide. Outside 2022–23 the effect is smaller, but first-published claims still add false ambers. Live readings use claims as first published, so treat a sudden claims-driven jump with some caution until the next revisions.

Why scores above 35% are shown in words

Below 35% the score is well calibrated in testing. Above it, it has been too confident: months scored 50% or more (average 76%) were followed by an event 56% of the time on today's claims figures and 44% on claims as first published. Each band rests on only 7–9 independent episodes. So the site shows "Elevated" for 35–50% and "High" for 50% and above, with the number only in the tooltip.

What the forecast ranges can and can't do

Each range mixes two things: a "quiet times" path fitted on months with no event near, and the paths that followed past events, weighted by the score's probability for the matching window. When risk rises, the upper side of the range stretches toward past downturns, so the fan is lopsided. The ranges held about the right share of outcomes in testing overall, and the middle line beat "no change" by 13–27%. They were weakest when it mattered most: in forecasts made once an event was under way, the 80% range for unemployment held only about two-thirds to three-quarters of outcomes, and in the 2008–09 downturn the middle line ran 0.2 to 0.6 point too low. During a downturn, treat the upper edge of the range as a floor for planning, and look at the what-if paths. But professional forecasters' consensus has been a better single-number forecast than our middle line (about 15–20% lower error): use the range and the tilt, not the middle line. In 2022–26 unemployment drifted up slowly without an event, and the middle line ran 0.1–0.3 point below what happened; across all late-cycle periods since 2000 there is no such lean, so it was not corrected. The 12-month range is lower confidence.

2020

The pandemic was not foreseeable from these inputs. The score was 51% in December 2019 (a claims bump), 5% in February 2020 and 97% at the end of March. 2020 is left out of every fit, and every forecast range failed that year: past events hold nothing like a 25-fold jump in claims.

Limits
  • Since 2000 the test holds two recessions (2001, 2008–09), five scares and 2020: a small sample.
  • Scares count as events, so some "false" warnings in a recession-only sense are real warnings here.
  • Values as first published exist only from about 2011 for temp help, the factory surveys and the NFCI (claims: today's figures throughout); earlier tests use revised values, which flatters them somewhat.
  • Event dates come from final data; a new event is added only after review.
  • Some design choices (which inputs, the weight constraint, the colour rules) were made after seeing the 2000–2026 results, so those results lean favourable. The live record, kept since November 2026, is the clean test.
  • The model is frozen between January re-fits: weekly runs plug new data into the same weights.
  • The score does not measure the duration of unemployment. Continuing claims are an input but have never carried weight beside initial claims.
