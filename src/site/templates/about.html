About Unemployment Watch

An early-warning view of US unemployment built from public data (one series is shown under its publisher's citation terms). It follows two things: new job losses (how often people start claiming state UI benefits) and how long unemployment lasts (how long they keep claiming). It refreshes itself every weekday morning.

Intended use: a regular briefing on labour-market conditions that affect unemployment, from public data. The early-warning score and forecast ranges are experimental model output with a short live record. They are a monitoring aid, not a basis on their own for pricing, reserving or risk limits.

Definitions

SA and NSA
Seasonally adjusted (SA) figures remove regular patterns such as January layoffs and holiday weeks, so one week can be compared with the next. Not seasonally adjusted (NSA) figures are the raw counts.
State UI claims
Claims for state unemployment insurance (UI) benefits, reported by each state to the U.S. Department of Labor (DOL). These are the "claims" throughout this site.
Initial claims
New applications for state UI benefits in a week: the fastest public signal of job losses.
Continuing claims
People still claiming benefits; reported for the week before the initial claims week, so they run a week behind. DOL and FRED call them “continued claims”.
State UI claims as a proxy
Initial claims are applications for state unemployment benefits; continuing claims are the number of people receiving them. Both depend on state eligibility rules, waiting periods and benefit limits. Continuing claims rise when more people start claiming as well as when people stay on benefits longer. For any private unemployment insurance, they are indicators of direction and timing, not measures of that insurance's own claims.
ETA 539
DOL's weekly claims report: national and state initial and continuing claims, covered employment and the insured unemployment rate. Published every Thursday with the news release; the official figures follow.
ETA 5159
DOL's monthly report of state UI claims and payments: first payments, weeks compensated, final payments (exhaustions) and amounts paid, by state.
ETA 218
DOL's quarterly report of benefit rights and experience: how many weeks new claimants are entitled to, and how many weeks claimants who exhausted benefits actually received, by state.
Covered employment
The number of jobs covered by state unemployment insurance (about 154 million), published by the Department of Labor (DOL). It is the denominator of the two claims rates.
SA initial claims rate
Seasonally adjusted initial claims per 10,000 covered workers, weekly. Example: 189,000 claims ÷ 153.9 million covered workers × 10,000 = 12.3.
SA continuing claims rate
Seasonally adjusted continuing claims as a percent of covered workers. It reproduces DOL's published insured unemployment rate: the largest difference in any week since {{ iur_since }} is {{ iur_max_diff }} percentage points, which is DOL's rounding to one decimal.
4-week average
The mean of the latest four weeks, which smooths holiday and weather noise. Charts show it bold, with the weekly figures faint behind it.
Preliminary weeks (advance, revised)
The newest weeks come from DOL's Thursday news release: the latest week is the advance figure, the week before is revised. They are drawn dashed with hollow markers and are replaced when DOL's official weekly report (ETA 539) covers them, usually within a few weeks.
Exhaustion rate (12-month)
The share of claimants who use up all their state benefits: 12-month average final payments ÷ 12-month average first payments six months earlier (DOL's definition).
Average benefit duration
Weeks of benefits paid per claimant: 12-month weeks compensated ÷ 12-month first payments, for all claimants. Shown against the average number of weeks claimants are entitled to (DOL's ETA 218 report).
Percentile
The share of all past observations at or below the latest value, over the full history shown on the tile or table (for example "62nd percentile since 1971"). At the extremes the site says "Lowest since 1971 (start of data)": the data begin there, so it is not an all-time record.
Sahm indicator
The 3-month average unemployment rate minus the lowest 3-month average of the prior 12 months. Readings of 0.50 or more have come in the early months of most past US recessions (St. Louis Fed, real-time version).
Event
A claims jump (new claims 25% or more above their low of the past year) or an unemployment rise (3-month average 0.5 point above its low of the past year). About half of past events were short scares.
Recession shading
Grey bands mark recessions as dated by the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER). NBER announces dates months after the fact, so a new downturn appears only once it is official.
Diffusion index
Regional Fed surveys report the share of firms seeing increases minus the share seeing decreases; below zero means more firms report declines. The States page uses a different one, the state claims diffusion index: the share of covered jobs in states (and DC) whose state UI initial claims, as a 4-week average, are up 20% or more on the same weeks a year earlier. Each state counts by its covered employment; the simple share of states is in the download. The 20% threshold was fixed before any testing.
NFCI
The Chicago Fed's National Financial Conditions Index: zero is the average since 1971, positive means tighter than average. The credit subindex covers credit conditions only.

Considered but not used

  • Revelio Labs Public Labor Statistics (RPLS): monthly employment, hiring, attrition and layoff-notice estimates from online profiles. Revelio's Terms of Service do not allow its content to be posted on another website without written consent, or downloaded automatically, so it is not shown. ADP private payrolls (Job Losses page) serve as the second, non-government read on jobs instead.
  • WARN layoff notices (advance notices employers must file before large layoffs): planned as a context chart; not yet added, because the free sources that compile them need a registered account. The Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland researchers' compilation (published under a Creative Commons licence) is the preferred source.
  • Google Trends (searches for "file for unemployment" and similar): no licensed automated access (the official API is in a closed test), and this site does not scrape.
  • Challenger, Gray & Christmas job-cut announcements: no public terms for reuse.
  • Indeed, ISM and Conference Board indicators: not free to republish.

How often it updates

  • The site rebuilds itself on weekday mornings at about 7:45 and 10:00 am Central Time, after the morning releases.
  • On Thursdays an extra run starts at about 7:35 am CT and waits up to an hour for the weekly claims release.
  • The early-warning score and forecast ranges are recomputed automatically by the first refresh after each Thursday claims release and each jobs report (other days the last run stays), with weights fixed for the year. Two steps are manual: the January re-fit of the weights is reviewed before it is used, and a new event (a claims jump or unemployment rise) is added to the history only after review. If a model run fails or a release is late, the pages say so and keep the last good values. How it works: Outlook → Methodology.
  • The ⚠ stale badge next to "Data through" appears if a series is older than its normal release schedule allows (for example, a missed weekly claims release).

Revisions

  • Publishers revise their figures. Charts, tiles and downloads always show the latest published values.
  • For FRED and Chicago Fed series the project also keeps a dated copy each time a series changes, so later analysis can use only what was known at the time.
  • DOL files keep only the latest figures; every revision DOL makes to past weeks, months or quarters is recorded in the project's data revision log.

Method notes

  • US totals from state reports. The US exhaustion rate and average benefit duration are sums over all states, used only for months (quarters) in which every state reported. Our state figures match DOL's published ones for the states we checked (Alabama, California, Florida, New York, Ohio, Texas), but the US total can differ slightly from DOL's published US figure (0.08 percentage points for exhaustion and 0.02 weeks for duration in the 2026 Q2 comparison).
  • Why claims rates start in 1971. The rates divide claims by covered employment, and DOL's weekly report publishes covered employment from 1971 (claims themselves go back to 1967). In 1978 UI coverage expanded to most state and local government workers, so covered employment jumped and rates before and after 1978 are not strictly comparable; the rate charts mark it. DOL's state files (ETA 539 by state, 5159, 218) start in 1995 by choice of this project.
  • State measures (States page). State claims are regular state UI programs only, not seasonally adjusted: DOL publishes no state seasonal factors, and applying the national factors to states would be wrong because states' seasonal patterns differ. So each week is compared with the same weeks a year earlier. Every year-on-year figure on the States page uses one basis: the claims rate (claims per 100 covered jobs, 4-week average), for each state, for National (the sum of the states and DC) and for Weighted; the continuing claims rate changes in points. "vs. normal" compares the claims rate with the median of the same weeks in the prior 3 years, leaving out March 2020 to June 2021. DOL's national figures and FRED's state claims series also add work-sharing claims (and the national ones Puerto Rico and the Virgin Islands), so they run slightly above ours. The state "vs. 12-month low" gap follows the Sahm rule: the 3-month average of the state unemployment rate minus its lowest 3-month average of the previous 12 months; no confirmation trigger is attached to it, and the "rising states" list is a watch list that has not been tested on history. The map, tiles and table show the last week or month at or before the end of the time setting in which nearly all states (90% or more) had reported; a state that has not reported that period shows its own latest value with its date, or "not reported".
  • Weighted view (States page). {{ weighted_sentence }} The weighted figures are each state's value times its weight, summed; when a state has no figure for a period, the remaining weights are scaled to sum to one. The weighted % change in claims is the sum of the states' contributions (weight × change in the state's claims rate ÷ the weighted rate a year earlier). The weighted series can be downloaded on the Data page.
